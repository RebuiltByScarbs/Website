The Comeback Process

Training back from injury: the order that actually matters.

9 MIN READ · THE REBUILD

Here's the version nobody wants to hear in week one: your enthusiasm is not the limiting factor. Tissue capacity is. You can want to squat your old numbers as badly as you like — tendons, cartilage and a deconditioned nervous system do not care, and they will send the bill later, usually around week four, right when you've finally built some momentum back.

Most reinjuries I see aren't from training too little. They're from getting the order wrong — doing the right things in the wrong sequence, which from the outside looks identical to doing it right, right up until it isn't.

The four stages, and why the order is non-negotiable

This is the same structure I run with every 1:1 client, because it's the same structure rehab and strength-and-conditioning literature has converged on for decades: graded exposure before intensity, capacity before load, load before performance.

  1. Audit. What actually happened, what's actually healed, and what your last attempt at a comeback got wrong. Skip this and you're just repeating whatever failed last time with better intentions.
  2. Rebuild the base. Volume and frequency go up before intensity does. This is the boring stage. It's also the one people cut short, which is almost always where things go wrong.
  3. Load it back on. Progressive overload resumes properly — once there's a base that can actually absorb it — alongside nutrition dialled to the goal and a setback protocol already written down, before you need it.
  4. Make it boring. The finish line isn't a PR. It's a routine you stop having to think about, where a missed week is an inconvenience, not a crisis.
Capacity before load, every time. The people who skip stage two aren't more committed than everyone else — they just haven't been bitten by it yet.

What this looks like practically, in week one

If you're newly back after injury or a long layoff, week one should feel almost suspiciously manageable. Submaximal loads, generous recovery between sessions, and — this is the part people hate — deliberately stopping sets with reps in the tank rather than grinding. If week one leaves you wrecked for four days, it wasn't calibrated to where you actually are, it was calibrated to where you used to be. Those are not the same training plan, even if the exercises look identical on paper.

A rough gut-check: if you can't hold a conversation through most of a session in week one, you've started too hot. That's not a vibes-based rule, it's just a cheap proxy for staying submaximal while your tissues and your nervous system both recalibrate.

The bit that actually derails people

It's rarely the training itself. It's impatience with stage two — rebuilding the base feels like nothing is happening, so people either bail or quietly add intensity back in before capacity is there to support it. Then something tweaks, and the story becomes "my body just can't handle training any more," when the honest version is "I skipped a stage."

If you only take one thing from this: write down which of the four stages you're actually in before every session, not which one you wish you were in. It's the single cheapest way to stop re-injuring yourself on the way back.
