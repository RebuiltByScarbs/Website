Identity & Mindset

"I used to be in shape" is a trap, not a fact.

6 MIN READ · THE REBUILD

Say it out loud: "I used to be in shape." Feels harmless. It's not — it's a verdict. The second you file your fitness under past tense, every decision after that point gets judged against who you were, not who you're trying to become. You skip a session and it "proves" the old you is gone for good. You have a bad week and it confirms the story. The sentence isn't a description any more, it's running the show.

I said it for about eighteen months, somewhere between injury number two and the point I was 30kg heavier than I wanted to admit. It didn't motivate me once. It just gave me a reason to feel bad without doing anything differently.

Why your brain likes this sentence so much

Self-perception theory — the idea that we often infer who we are by watching our own behaviour, not the other way round — has been kicking around psychology since the early 1970s. The practical version: you don't just act according to your identity, your identity gets built from what you've been doing lately. Which is exactly the problem. If "lately" means eight months off, your brain has quietly updated the file. Not "person who trains and is in a rough patch." Just: not a trainer any more.

That's also why going back in guns blazing — the classic Monday reset with a program built for the person you used to be — fails so often. You're not fighting a lack of knowledge. You're fighting a self-concept that hasn't caught up, and it will sabotage a plan that doesn't account for it.

The fix isn't a pep talk. It's evidence. Identity updates on repeated action, not on how badly you want it to.

The reframe that actually works

Swap "I used to be in shape" for "I'm rebuilding." Not because it sounds nicer — because it's mechanically different. One is a closed story. The other is a process you're currently inside of, which means today's session counts as evidence for it instead of a referendum on whether you've failed.

This is also, not coincidentally, the entire logic behind habit-identity work (the "every action is a vote for the type of person you want to be" framing popularised by James Clear draws on the same self-perception research). Small, repeatable, boring actions change the evidence pile faster than one heroic comeback week ever will.

What to actually do about it

  • Stop auditing yourself against 2019-you. That person isn't walking through the door for this program, you are — present tense, with whatever's healed and whatever hasn't.
  • Pick the smallest version of "training" that still counts as a vote. Three sessions you'll actually do beats five you'll abandon by week two.
  • Track streaks, not PRs, for the first few weeks. Identity responds to consistency long before your lifts do.

None of this is complicated. It's just not what your brain wants to hear when it's already decided the story's over.
